# Parti Strategies — Deep Reference

Comprehensive reference on 16 archetypal parti strategies for architectural design. Each entry documents the strategy's diagrammatic logic, historical lineage, structural implications, environmental characteristics, programmatic applications, scale range, and built examples.

---

## 1. The Bar (Single Linear Volume)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A single elongated rectangular volume with a length-to-width ratio of 3:1 or greater. The bar is the simplest inhabited form — a wall thickened to contain program. Circulation runs parallel to the long axis, either along one edge (single-loaded) or through the center (double-loaded).

**Historical Lineage:** The bar is perhaps the most ancient and persistent building form. Le Corbusier's Unite d'Habitation (1952) is the canonical modern bar — 137 m long, 24 m deep, 56 m tall. Mies van der Rohe's Lafayette Park rowhouses (1963) demonstrated the bar at domestic scale. Alison and Peter Smithson's Robin Hood Gardens (1972) deployed two bars defining a communal garden.

**Structural Implications:** Short spans perpendicular to the long axis (6-15 m). Load-bearing cross-walls at 3.6-7.5 m centers (residential) or frame structure at 6-9 m bays (commercial). Lateral stability from cross-walls, end cores, or braced bays every 20-40 m. Expansion joints every 40-60 m in RC. Foundations: continuous strip or pad footings.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Optimal for cross-ventilation (building depth 12-15 m maximum for natural ventilation). South-facing facade (in northern hemisphere) receives maximum solar gain. North-facing facade provides even, glare-free daylight. Daylight penetration: 2-2.5x ceiling height from each facade. A 14 m deep bar with 3.0 m ceilings achieves daylight to the center.

**Program Types Best Served:** Residential apartments (single-aspect: 6-8 m depth; dual-aspect: 12-15 m), school classroom wings (8-9 m depth), hospital wards (15-18 m depth with central corridor), laboratory buildings (service spine with labs on each side), linear galleries.

**Scale Range:** From a single-story house (3 m wide x 20 m long) to a megastructure (Unite: 137 m x 24 m x 56 m). Most effective at medium scale: 60-150 m long, 12-18 m deep, 3-10 stories.

**Built Examples:**
- Unite d'Habitation, Marseille (Le Corbusier, 1952) — 337 apartments, internal street at mid-height, rooftop community facilities, Modulor-proportioned interiors
- Narkomfin Building, Moscow (Ginzburg, 1930) — 85 m long, skip-stop corridor, duplex apartments oriented east-west, communal services in a detached block connected by a bridge
- Tietgen Student Hall, Copenhagen (Lundgaard & Tranberg, 2006) — while circular in plan, its residential wings are organized as curved bars, each 7.2 m deep with single-loaded corridor

## 2. The Courtyard (Perimeter Block with Central Void)

**Diagrammatic Description:** Program arranged around one or more enclosed open-air spaces. The courtyard is a subtracted void — the negative space IS the design move. Rooms face inward toward the controlled microclimate of the courtyard.

**Historical Lineage:** Among the oldest architectural strategies, found across all cultures: the Roman domus (atrium and peristyle), the Chinese siheyuan, the Islamic riad, the Spanish patio house. In modern architecture: Louis Kahn's Salk Institute (1965), Aldo van Eyck's Amsterdam Orphanage (1960), David Chipperfield's Am Kupfergraben 10 (2007).

**Structural Implications:** Load-bearing perimeter walls or frame structure along all four sides. Corner conditions are critical: either column at corner, or cantilevered slabs from adjacent walls. Courtyard dimensions affect structural spans: 15-30 m courtyards allow economical RC framing; larger courtyards may need bridges or covered connections. Perimeter depth: 6-12 m (single-loaded) to 12-18 m (double-loaded).

**Environmental Characteristics:** Courtyard microclimate varies with proportions. In hot-arid climates: narrow, deep courtyards (height:width 2:1 to 3:1) provide self-shading and cool air pooling at night. In temperate climates: wider courtyards (height:width 1:2 to 1:1) admit maximum winter sun. Courtyards reduce wind speed by 50-70% compared to exposed facades. Cross-ventilation from courtyard to exterior facade is highly effective.

**Program Types Best Served:** Housing (all scales, all climates), schools (courtyard as playground), monasteries and convents, museums (courtyard as outdoor gallery), offices (courtyard as social space), hotels (courtyard as garden oasis).

**Scale Range:** From a single-family courtyard house (8 m x 8 m courtyard) to a city block (100 m x 100 m perimeter block with 60 m x 60 m courtyard). Optimal courtyard size: 15-40 m across for residential; 20-60 m for institutional.

**Built Examples:**
- Salk Institute, La Jolla (Kahn, 1965) — two parallel laboratory wings flanking a 61 m x 20 m travertine courtyard open to the Pacific; water channel on center axis; study towers between labs
- Alhambra, Granada (14th century) — Court of the Myrtles (36.6 m x 23.5 m), reflecting pool, arcade on short ends; Court of the Lions (28.5 m x 15.7 m), columned arcade, central fountain with 12 marble lions
- Am Kupfergraben 10, Berlin (Chipperfield, 2007) — residential courtyard block, 6-story, limestone and precast concrete, courtyard 18 m x 12 m, views to Museum Island

## 3. The Cruciform (Cross-Shaped Plan)

**Diagrammatic Description:** Two bars intersecting at right angles, creating a cross. The intersection is the spatial and structural climax. Four wings extend in cardinal (or near-cardinal) directions.

**Historical Lineage:** The Christian basilica established the cruciform as the default church plan (nave + transepts + chancel). Renaissance architects (Bramante, Michelangelo) developed the Greek cross (equal arms) for centralized churches. In secular architecture: Mies van der Rohe's cruciform columns, Le Corbusier's Villa Savoye ramp system, and OMA's Kunsthal, Rotterdam (1992).

**Structural Implications:** The intersection carries the highest loads (four wings converging). Requires robust structure at the crossing: heavy columns, transfer beams, or a dome/vault. Wing spans are independent of each other. The crossing can be expressed as a tower, lantern, dome, or void.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Each wing can be oriented differently for solar optimization. North wing: studios/offices (diffuse light). South wing: living spaces (direct sun with shading). East wing: bedrooms (morning light). West wing: service/storage (minimize glazing). The intersection can function as a stack-effect ventilation hub if designed as a tall void.

**Program Types Best Served:** Churches and cathedrals (nave + transepts + chancel + crossing), hospitals (four wings from a central nursing station), villas (four wings for different zones: living, sleeping, service, guest), schools (four classroom wings from central commons).

**Scale Range:** From a small chapel (15 m x 15 m footprint) to a cathedral (150 m x 60 m). Most effective at medium-large institutional scale.

**Built Examples:**
- St. Peter's Basilica, Rome (Bramante/Michelangelo/Maderno, 1626) — Greek cross plan extended to Latin cross, 220 m long, dome 42 m diameter at crossing, 136 m to lantern top
- Kunsthal, Rotterdam (OMA, 1992) — cruciform circulation: ramp and path cross at center, four quadrants with different programs and spatial characters
- Swiss Re Headquarters "The Gherkin," London (Foster, 2004) — while circular in plan, the structural diagrid creates a cruciform pattern at the base where it meets the ground

## 4. The Pinwheel (Rotational Asymmetry)

**Diagrammatic Description:** Three or four elements rotating around a central pivot without touching at the center. Each element extends outward and slightly past the next, creating a windmill-like rotational energy. The center is a void or threshold — not a room.

**Historical Lineage:** Mies van der Rohe's Brick Country House project (1924) established the pinwheel as a modern architectural strategy. The Barcelona Pavilion (1929) is its built apotheosis: walls slide past each other, never enclosing space completely. Gerrit Rietveld's Schroder House (1924) uses a pinwheel composition of colored planes. Frank Lloyd Wright's Usonian houses employ pinwheel plans radiating from a central masonry core.

**Structural Implications:** No single load-bearing core at center — loads are distributed to perimeter walls and columns. Cantilevers at element terminations are essential for the visual effect of rotation (2-4 m typical). Roof planes may be independent (expressing each element's autonomy) or continuous (unifying the composition). Steel or timber frame is preferred for the slender profiles required.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Each element can be oriented independently for optimal sun/wind/view response. The central void provides natural light and ventilation to the heart of the plan. Pinwheel plans typically have excellent cross-ventilation (multiple exposures per room). The open center prevents deep-plan conditions.

**Program Types Best Served:** Single-family houses, villas, pavilions, small cultural buildings, visitor centers, meditation spaces. The pinwheel works best at small-to-medium scale where the rotational composition is perceptible.

**Scale Range:** From a garden pavilion (50 m2) to a medium-scale house (300-500 m2). Above 500 m2, the pinwheel becomes difficult to read and loses its dynamic quality.

**Built Examples:**
- Barcelona Pavilion (Mies, 1929) — walls of onyx, Tinian marble, and chrome-clad steel slide past each other in a pinwheel around an open-air pool; 8 cruciform columns independent of wall planes
- Schroder House, Utrecht (Rietveld, 1924) — colored planes (red, blue, yellow, grey) projecting and receding in a three-dimensional pinwheel; upper floor transforms from open plan to 4 rooms via sliding partitions
- Pope-Leighey House, Virginia (Wright, 1941) — Usonian pinwheel: masonry utility core as pivot, living wing and bedroom wing extending at right angles, carport as third element

## 5. The Campus (Distributed Pavilions)

**Diagrammatic Description:** Multiple discrete buildings arranged on a shared landscape. Each pavilion houses a distinct function. The landscape between pavilions is as important as the buildings themselves. Circulation is primarily exterior.

**Historical Lineage:** The ancient Greek agora, the Roman forum, the medieval monastery, the Oxford/Cambridge college, and the American university campus. In modern architecture: Mies van der Rohe's IIT Campus (1940-58), Alvar Aalto's Saynatsalo Town Hall (1952), and Renzo Piano's Jean-Marie Tjibaou Cultural Centre (1998).

**Structural Implications:** Each pavilion is structurally independent, allowing different structural systems per building (e.g., mass timber for low-rise, steel for long-span, RC for multi-story). No transfer structures between buildings. Connections (covered walkways, bridges) are lightweight and thermally separated.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Excellent natural ventilation (all facades exposed). Maximum daylight (no deep-plan conditions). Buildings can be individually oriented for solar optimization. Landscape between buildings provides cooling, stormwater management, and biodiversity. Energy penalties: high surface-area-to-volume ratio increases heat loss in cold climates; mitigated by compact individual pavilion forms.

**Program Types Best Served:** Universities (pavilion per department), corporate campuses (Apple Park: pavilions within a ring), cultural complexes (Jean-Marie Tjibaou: 10 case/pavilions), healthcare villages, school campuses, museum complexes (Museum Island, Berlin).

**Scale Range:** From a small compound (3-5 pavilions on 0.5 hectares) to a large campus (50+ buildings on 100+ hectares). The campus strategy scales almost infinitely through phased growth.

**Built Examples:**
- IIT Campus, Chicago (Mies, 1940-58) — steel and glass pavilions on a 7.3 m grid, 120-acre campus, Crown Hall as centerpiece (36.6 m x 67 m clear span)
- Vitra Campus, Weil am Rhein (multiple architects, 1981-present) — factory, museum, fire station, conference pavilion by Hadid, Gehry, Ando, Herzog & de Meuron, SANAA — each a distinct architectural statement on shared landscape
- Apple Park, Cupertino (Foster, 2017) — 260,000 m2 ring building encircling a 12-hectare landscape, but the campus also includes discrete pavilions: theater, fitness center, visitor center

## 6. The Tower (Vertical Extrusion)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A compact floor plate extruded vertically. The core (elevators, stairs, risers, restrooms) is the organizational spine. All occupied space is within 9-15 m of the perimeter (the "leasing depth" in commercial towers).

**Historical Lineage:** The medieval tower house, the campanile, the minaret, and the lighthouse are ancestral forms. The modern tower begins with the Chicago School (Home Insurance Building, Jenney, 1885) and matures with Mies (Lake Shore Drive, 1951), SOM (Lever House, 1952), and Fazlur Khan's structural innovations (tubular structures at SOM: John Hancock Center, 1969; Sears Tower, 1973).

**Structural Implications:** Core provides gravity load path and lateral resistance. Systems by height: rigid frame (up to 20 stories), braced core (up to 40 stories), tube-in-tube (up to 60 stories), bundled tube (up to 110 stories), outrigger (up to 100+ stories), diagrid (up to 80 stories). Floor plate efficiency (net-to-gross): 75-82% for commercial, 80-88% for residential. Structural premium: 1-2% of total cost below 40 stories; 5-10% at 40-60 stories; 15-25% above 60 stories.

**Environmental Characteristics:** High wind loads at upper floors (wind pressure increases with height squared). Stack effect in tall buildings creates air infiltration challenges (neutral pressure plane at approximately mid-height). Limited natural ventilation above 8-10 stories due to wind speed. High surface-area-to-volume ratio relative to floor area. Solar exposure is excellent but requires high-performance glazing (target: U-value 1.1-1.6 W/m2K, SHGC 0.25-0.40 for commercial towers).

**Program Types Best Served:** Commercial offices (500-2,500 m2 floor plates), residential apartments (400-1,200 m2 floor plates), hotels (600-1,500 m2 floor plates), mixed-use (office + residential + hotel stacked with sky lobbies).

**Scale Range:** From a 10-story residential tower (30 m) to a 160+ story supertall (828 m, Burj Khalifa). Most economically efficient at 20-50 stories for commercial, 30-70 stories for residential.

**Built Examples:**
- Seagram Building, New York (Mies + Johnson, 1958) — 38 stories, 157 m, bronze I-beam mullions, 27 m plaza setback from Park Avenue, 40% site coverage
- Commerzbank, Frankfurt (Foster, 1997) — 53 stories, 259 m, triangular plan with central atrium, 4-story sky gardens on each face in rotation, natural ventilation for 60% of occupied hours
- Bosco Verticale, Milan (Boeri, 2014) — 110 m and 76 m towers, 900 trees and 20,000 plants on staggered balconies, irrigation system from greywater recycling

## 7. The Podium-Tower (Hybrid Base + Vertical)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A horizontal base (podium) of 2-6 stories supporting one or more vertical towers. The podium addresses the street and houses large-floor-plate programs; the tower rises above and houses small-floor-plate programs.

**Historical Lineage:** The podium-tower emerged from mid-20th century zoning codes that required street-level engagement with tower setbacks above. Lever House (SOM, 1952) pioneered the type. It became the default mixed-use urban form in Asian megacities (Hong Kong, Singapore, Bangkok).

**Structural Implications:** Transfer structure at the podium-tower interface is critical. Tower columns at 6-9 m spacing must transfer to podium columns at 8-12 m spacing (for parking or retail below). Transfer beams: 1.2-3.0 m deep RC or steel plate girders. Transfer plates: 600-1,200 mm RC flat slab. Podium lateral system: shear walls or braced frames. Tower lateral system: core walls.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Podium roof as amenity terrace (pool, garden, play area) — must be waterproofed and insulated as an occupied roof (inverted warm roof: insulation above membrane). Podium shelters pedestrians from tower-generated downwash winds. Tower above podium has full 360-degree solar exposure.

**Program Types Best Served:** Mixed-use urban development (retail podium + residential tower), hotels above convention centers, transit-oriented development (transit station at podium base), institutional (library/commons podium + office/residential tower).

**Scale Range:** Podium: 2-6 stories, 2,000-20,000 m2 per floor. Tower: 10-80 stories, 400-2,500 m2 per floor. Total GFA: 10,000-500,000 m2.

**Built Examples:**
- Marina Bay Sands, Singapore (Safdie, 2010) — 3 towers (57 stories, 200 m) on a 5-story podium (casino, convention, retail, theater), connected by 340 m SkyPark at roof level
- VIA 57 West, New York (BIG, 2016) — courtyard-tower hybrid, podium engages the waterfront, pyramidal massing maximizes light and views, 709 residential units
- Pacific Place, Hong Kong (Wong & Ouyang, 1988) — 4-story retail podium with covered atrium, 3 hotel towers and 1 office tower above, 100,000+ m2 GFA

## 8. The Megastructure (Infrastructure + Infill)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A large-scale permanent structure (frame, megacolumns, service cores, horizontal platforms) that supports smaller-scale, replaceable, or adaptable infill units. The megastructure provides infrastructure; the infill provides habitation.

**Historical Lineage:** Yona Friedman's Ville Spatiale (1958), Archigram's Plug-In City (1964), Kisho Kurokawa's Nakagin Capsule Tower (1972), and Moshe Safdie's Habitat 67 (1967). The concept was theorized by Fumihiko Maki in "Investigations in Collective Form" (1964) and Reyner Banham in "Megastructure: Urban Futures of the Recent Past" (1976).

**Structural Implications:** Primary structure: megacolumns (1.5-5 m diameter RC or steel composite), mega-trusses (3-6 m deep, spanning 30-60 m), service cores at regular intervals (60-100 m). Secondary structure: conventional framing within each infill unit. Connection between primary and secondary: standardized mechanical joints allowing unit removal/replacement.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Large thermal mass in primary structure. Infill units can be individually climate-controlled. Open structure allows air movement through the megastructure. Risk of overheating in enclosed megastructure volumes — require stack-effect ventilation or mechanical air handling.

**Program Types Best Served:** Mixed-use urban complexes, transit hubs, mega-events (Olympic villages), incremental housing (infrastructure first, units added over time), disaster relief (rapid deployment of standardized units on permanent frame).

**Scale Range:** By definition, megastructures are large: minimum 10,000 m2, typically 50,000-500,000+ m2. Height: 20-100+ m. Length: 100-1,000+ m.

**Built Examples:**
- Habitat 67, Montreal (Safdie, 1967) — 354 prefabricated concrete boxes (17.2 m3 each), stacked in interlocking configurations, 158 apartments, each with private terrace on the roof of the unit below
- Nakagin Capsule Tower, Tokyo (Kurokawa, 1972) — 140 capsules (2.3 m x 3.8 m x 2.1 m) bolted to 2 interconnected concrete towers, designed for 25-year replacement cycle (never replaced; demolished 2022)
- The Interlace, Singapore (OMA, 2013) — 31 apartment blocks (6 stories each) stacked in a hexagonal arrangement, 1,040 units, 170,000 m2, sky gardens at block intersections

## 9. The Atrium (Vertical Void as Organizer)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A central or offset multi-story void surrounded by occupied floors on multiple levels. The atrium is the spatial and social heart — all movement, sightlines, and daylight converge on this void.

**Historical Lineage:** The Roman atrium house (compluvium/impluvium), the medieval great hall, the Victorian arcade and department store, and the modern atrium hotel (John Portman's Hyatt Regency Atlanta, 1967). The Ford Foundation (Roche Dinkeloo, 1968) introduced the atrium to office design.

**Structural Implications:** Long-span roof structure over atrium (trusses, space frames, cable-net, ETFE cushions). Floor plates span from perimeter to atrium edge — cantilevers or beams. Atrium glazing: structural glass walls (spider fittings on cable trusses) or framed curtain walls. Smoke management: minimum 2 m smoke reservoir at atrium top, mechanical smoke extract per BS 9999 or IBC Section 404.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Daylight delivered deep into plan via atrium glazing (atrium floor target: 2% daylight factor minimum). Stack effect drives natural ventilation (atrium height creates pressure differential: 1 Pa per 3 m height in warm conditions). Risk of overheating in glazed atriums — require solar shading (horizontal louvers or frit patterns, target < 50% SHGC on atrium glazing) and ventilation openings at top.

**Program Types Best Served:** Hotels (atrium as dramatic lobby), offices (atrium as social condenser), shopping centers (atrium as orientation device), hospitals (atrium for wayfinding and daylight), universities (atrium as informal learning/social space), civic buildings.

**Scale Range:** Small atrium: 6 m x 6 m x 3 stories (residential). Medium: 15 m x 30 m x 6-10 stories (office/hotel). Large: 30 m x 100 m x 15+ stories (hotel/retail). The Burj Al Arab atrium is 180 m tall.

**Built Examples:**
- Ford Foundation, New York (Roche Dinkeloo, 1968) — 12-story planted atrium, L-shaped office floors on two sides, first modern office building with atrium garden
- Commerzbank, Frankfurt (Foster, 1997) — 53-story triangular plan, central atrium with 4-story sky gardens rotating around three faces, natural ventilation via atrium stack effect
- Bradbury Building, Los Angeles (Wyman, 1893) — 5-story skylit atrium, ornamental iron railings, open-cage elevators, 15 m wide, Italian Renaissance proportions

## 10. The Ring (Continuous Perimeter)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A continuous band of occupied space forming a closed loop around a central landscape, courtyard, or void. Unlike the courtyard type (which can be rectangular with distinct sides), the ring is a singular continuous gesture.

**Historical Lineage:** Ancient amphitheaters (Colosseum: 188 m x 156 m ellipse, 80 arched bays), medieval city walls, and 19th-century ring roads (Vienna Ringstrasse). In modern architecture: Alvar Aalto's Baker House at MIT (1949, serpentine bar as partial ring), Oscar Niemeyer's National Congress complex. Apple Park (Foster, 2017) is the definitive contemporary ring.

**Structural Implications:** Curved structure: RC or steel frame following the ring geometry. For true circles: radial columns, ring beams, and circumferential floor plates. Construction efficiency depends on radius — large radii approximate straight segments. Expansion joints required at intervals (every 30-40 m of arc length). Foundation: continuous ring footing or piles.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Central landscape is protected from wind and noise. The ring building itself acts as a sound and wind barrier. Solar exposure varies continuously around the ring — every orientation is present. Large rings (> 100 m diameter) enclose significant landscape (Apple Park: 12 hectares within the ring).

**Program Types Best Served:** Corporate headquarters (egalitarian — no corner offices), sports stadiums and arenas, exhibition buildings, cultural centers, large-scale housing.

**Scale Range:** From a small ring house (20 m diameter, 200 m2) to a mega-ring (Apple Park: 461 m diameter, 260,000 m2). Structural and construction complexity increases with diameter.

**Built Examples:**
- Apple Park, Cupertino (Foster, 2017) — 461 m diameter ring, 260,000 m2, 4 stories, curved glass panels (800+ unique shapes), 100% renewable energy, 12-hectare central garden
- Colosseum, Rome (80 CE) — 188 m x 156 m ellipse, 48.5 m high, 80 entrance arches, 50,000 spectators, travertine and tufa structure with concrete vaults
- Tulou Housing, Guangzhou (Urbanus, 2008) — modern reinterpretation of Fujian tulou (circular earthen houses), 72 m diameter, 6 stories, 220 low-income apartments around central courtyard

## 11. The Slab (Deep-Plan Horizontal Volume)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A wide, relatively flat building with a depth exceeding 25 m. Unlike the bar (12-18 m deep), the slab relies on top-lighting, light wells, or artificial illumination for its interior zones.

**Historical Lineage:** The factory building (daylight sawtooth roofs), the department store (lightwells), the mid-century office (deep-plan with fluorescent lighting), and contemporary big-box retail and logistics. In architecture: the Sainsbury Centre (Foster, 1978) — 35 m wide clear-span shed; the Pompidou Centre (Piano + Rogers, 1977) — 48 m clear-span floors.

**Structural Implications:** Long-span structure required: steel trusses (30-60 m), pre-stressed concrete (20-40 m), space frames (40-100 m), or cable-stayed (50-150 m). Roof structure is a significant design element. Column-free interiors maximize flexibility. Floor loading: 5.0 kN/m2 for general office, 7.5 kN/m2 for library/archive, 10-25 kN/m2 for industrial.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Deep plans require top-lighting for natural daylight (sawtooth roofs, monitor roofs, skylights — target 3-5% of floor area as roof glazing). Mechanical ventilation is typically required for interior zones. High thermal mass (concrete slab-on-grade) provides thermal stability. Green roof potential on large flat roof areas.

**Program Types Best Served:** Museums and galleries (controlled top-light), factories and workshops, warehouses and logistics, supermarkets and retail, trading floors, sports facilities, convention/exhibition halls.

**Scale Range:** From a modest workshop (25 m x 50 m) to a mega-exhibition hall (100 m x 500 m+). The slab scales economically with prefabricated steel or precast concrete systems.

**Built Examples:**
- Sainsbury Centre, Norwich (Foster, 1978) — 35 m x 131 m, clear-span tubular steel trusses, adjustable wall panels for daylight and artificial light, art and academic spaces in a single volume
- Menil Collection, Houston (Piano, 1987) — ferro-cement "leaves" as ceiling/roof system, filtering and redirecting natural daylight onto gallery walls, 10,000 m2 single-story
- SANAA's Rolex Learning Center, Lausanne (2010) — 166 m x 121 m continuous undulating concrete slab with courtyards punched through, single-story, landscape-like interior

## 12. The Mat (Low-Rise, High-Density Field)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A continuous low-rise (1-4 stories) fabric covering a large site area with high density. The mat building blurs the boundary between architecture and urbanism — it is simultaneously a building and a piece of city. Courtyards, light wells, streets, and plazas are woven into the mat.

**Historical Lineage:** The Islamic medina (dense, low-rise, courtyard fabric), the medieval European town, and Alison Smithson's concept of "mat building" (1974). Free University Berlin (Candilis Josic Woods, 1973) is the canonical modern mat building. Fumihiko Maki's "group form" concept (1964) describes mat-like urban configurations.

**Structural Implications:** Repetitive low-rise structure: load-bearing walls, flat slabs, minimal column sizes (200-400 mm). Short spans (4-8 m) allow economical construction. Modular structural grids enable phased construction and future expansion. Transfer structures are rarely needed.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Excellent potential for natural ventilation and daylight via courtyards (every occupied space within 6-8 m of an external wall or courtyard). High thermal mass (concrete/masonry). Extensive green roofs possible (total roof area approximately equals site area). Low wind loads. Ground-coupled thermal performance.

**Program Types Best Served:** Universities (Free University Berlin), hospitals (low-rise campus model), housing (dense courtyard fabric), offices (campus-style), cultural complexes, souks and markets.

**Scale Range:** Minimum 5,000 m2 footprint to achieve mat character. Typical: 10,000-100,000 m2. Density: FAR 1.0-3.0 with 100% site coverage and 30-50% courtyard/void ratio.

**Built Examples:**
- Free University Berlin (Candilis Josic Woods, 1973) — Corten steel and glass, modular grid, covered internal streets (Strassen), courtyards at regular intervals, 70,000 m2, infinitely expandable in theory
- Amsterdam Orphanage (van Eyck, 1960) — tartan grid of domed units, interstitial courtyards, internal streets, domestic scale within institutional program
- Musee du Quai Branly, Paris (Nouvel, 2006) — while not a pure mat, its garden-integrated low-rise exhibition spaces exemplify mat-like qualities with 18,000 m2 on a long, narrow site

## 13. The Bridge (Spanning Structure as Inhabited Space)

**Diagrammatic Description:** A habitable volume that spans between two or more supports, creating covered space below and occupied space within the span. The bridge-building is simultaneously structure, enclosure, and gateway.

**Historical Lineage:** The Ponte Vecchio, Florence (1345) — shops spanning the Arno. The Bridge of Sighs, Venice (1600). Le Corbusier's Carpenter Center, Cambridge (1963) — ramp-as-bridge threading through the building. In contemporary architecture: CCTV Headquarters (OMA, 2012), The Bridge (Richard Rogers, 2004, Malpensa Airport).

**Structural Implications:** Bridge spans of 30-75 m require deep structure: Vierendeel trusses (2-4 m deep), box girders (1.5-3 m deep), or truss structures integrated into the building section. Support structures at each end carry heavy vertical and lateral loads. Wind loads on elevated spans are significant. Thermal movement in long spans requires expansion joints and sliding bearings.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Covered space below the bridge provides weather-protected public realm. The bridge itself has 360-degree exposure (excellent views but high solar gain and wind load). Elevated position captures views but also wind — requires high-performance glazing and potentially double-skin facade.

**Program Types Best Served:** Connections between campus buildings, transit infrastructure (stations, terminals), civic buildings making symbolic gestures across roads or rivers, corporate offices (bridging between departments).

**Scale Range:** From a modest link bridge (3 m wide x 20 m span) to a mega-bridge (CCTV: 75 m cantilever/span at 162 m height). Most bridge-buildings span 20-60 m.

**Built Examples:**
- CCTV Headquarters, Beijing (OMA/Arup, 2012) — two leaning towers (234 m, 194 m) connected by a 75 m cantilever at the top and a 9-story base, continuous loop circulation, diagrid facade
- Linked Hybrid, Beijing (Holl, 2009) — 8 residential towers connected at floors 12-18 by a ring of sky bridges containing pool, gym, cafe, gallery; 220,000 m2; bridges span 15-25 m
- Carpenter Center, Cambridge (Le Corbusier, 1963) — ramp-as-bridge threads through the building connecting two streets at different levels, pilotis, brise-soleil, Le Corbusier's only building in North America

## 14. The Split-Section (Interlocking Half-Levels)

**Diagrammatic Description:** Floor plates at half-story offsets connected by half-flights of stairs. The section is the primary generative drawing. Each space is sized in section according to its programmatic needs (tall for public, low for private).

**Historical Lineage:** Adolf Loos's Raumplan (Villa Muller, Prague, 1930) is the seminal precedent — each room at a different level, sized in height to its function. Le Corbusier's duplex units in the Unite d'Habitation use a split-section to create double-height living spaces with single-height bedrooms, accessed by a skip-stop corridor.

**Structural Implications:** Staggered floor slabs require bearing walls or columns at half-level offsets. Short spans (3-6 m) between bearing walls. Slabs at different levels create inherent lateral bracing. Foundation steps with the natural grade on sloping sites, reducing cut-and-fill. Structure is more complex to build than flat-slab construction — requires skilled formwork or precast panels.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Half-level offsets allow sightlines between spaces, creating visual openness without full double-height volumes. Natural ventilation benefits from section height variation (warm air rises to upper levels and is exhausted; cool air enters at lower levels). Daylight can penetrate deeper via level changes. Energy-efficient: no wasted volume — each space is exactly as tall as needed.

**Program Types Best Served:** Houses on slopes, duplexes and maisonettes, libraries (reading levels at half-stories), retail (browsing levels), small cultural buildings, hillside housing.

**Scale Range:** From a small house (100-300 m2) to a medium-scale housing block (5,000-15,000 m2 in skip-stop section). The split-section becomes impractical for accessibility above 4-5 stories without parallel elevator access.

**Built Examples:**
- Villa Muller, Prague (Loos, 1930) — cubic exterior conceals 10+ distinct levels within, each room at a height appropriate to its function (salon: 3.6 m, bedroom: 2.7 m, study: 2.4 m)
- Unite d'Habitation, Marseille (Le Corbusier, 1952) — skip-stop corridor at every third floor, duplex apartments with double-height living rooms (4.8 m) and single-height bedrooms (2.3 m), corridor: 2.6 m wide x 2.3 m high
- Gifu Kitagata Apartment Building (SANAA, 1998) — 10-story, 107 units, split-level sections creating terraces and double-height spaces within a repetitive structural grid

## 15. The Folded Plate (Section as Structure)

**Diagrammatic Description:** The building section IS the structural system — walls fold into roofs fold into walls. There is no distinction between vertical enclosure and horizontal span. The folded plate or folded surface resists loads through its geometry (shell action) rather than its mass.

**Historical Lineage:** Felix Candela's concrete shells (1950s-60s), Eero Saarinen's TWA Terminal (1962), and Jorn Utzon's Sydney Opera House (1973) established the folded/shell form. Contemporary digital tools enable complex folded plates: Foreign Office Architects' Yokohama International Ferry Terminal (2002), Zaha Hadid's MAXXI Museum (2010).

**Structural Implications:** Folded plates resist loads through membrane action — forces travel along the surface rather than through bending. Minimum fold angle: 15-20 degrees for structural efficiency. Typical material: reinforced concrete (150-250 mm shell thickness for 15-30 m spans), steel plate (8-20 mm), or CLT panels (100-200 mm). Edge beams at fold valleys carry concentrated loads. Foundations receive both vertical and horizontal thrust forces.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Folded forms can self-shade (one fold shades the adjacent surface). Rainwater collection is natural in fold valleys. The section can be designed to capture or deflect wind for natural ventilation. Acoustic character varies — concave folds can focus sound (problematic), convex folds can scatter it (beneficial).

**Program Types Best Served:** Transportation terminals (long-span canopy), cultural buildings (museums, concert halls), religious buildings (chapels, churches), sports facilities (grandstand canopies), pavilions and exhibition structures.

**Scale Range:** From a small pavilion (50 m2, 10 m span) to a major terminal (Yokohama Ferry Terminal: 430 m long). Folded plate efficiency improves with scale (larger spans benefit more from shell action).

**Built Examples:**
- Yokohama International Ferry Terminal (FOA, 2002) — 430 m long, folded steel plate landscape — ground surface folds up to become walls, then roof, then back to ground; no columns in main hall; origami-like structural logic
- TWA Terminal, JFK (Saarinen, 1962) — four intersecting concrete shells, 96 m x 96 m, shell thickness 180 mm at crown, interior as continuous flowing space
- MAXXI Museum, Rome (Hadid, 2010) — walls fold into ceilings, ceilings fold into ramps; cast-in-place concrete shells; 21,000 m2 of exhibition space in a continuous looping circuit

## 16. The Hybrid Stack (Programmatic Stacking)

**Diagrammatic Description:** Distinct programmatic zones stacked vertically, each with its own spatial character, structural bay, and facade expression. Unlike a tower (which repeats one floor plate), the hybrid stack changes character at each programmatic shift.

**Historical Lineage:** The traditional mixed-use urban building (shop at ground, office above, residence at top) is the ancestral form. OMA's Seattle Central Library (2004) formalized this as a design strategy — the "programmatic stack." Bjarke Ingels's LEGO House (2017) and The Mountain (2008) are contemporary exemplars of expressive programmatic stacking.

**Structural Implications:** Transfer structures at programmatic boundaries: columns shift grid at each program change. Transfer beams (1.5-3 m deep) or transfer plates (800-1,200 mm RC). Each program zone may require different floor-to-floor heights: retail: 4.5-6 m; office: 3.6-4.2 m; residential: 2.8-3.3 m; parking: 3.0-3.3 m. Structural engineers must resolve the load path through these transitions.

**Environmental Characteristics:** Each program zone can have tailored environmental systems: retail (high cooling loads, mechanical ventilation), office (mixed-mode, operable windows), residential (natural ventilation priority). Facade performance varies per zone: commercial zones require solar control glazing; residential zones benefit from operable windows and balconies. Sky gardens at programmatic transitions provide amenity and ventilation breaks.

**Program Types Best Served:** Mixed-use urban buildings (any combination of retail, office, residential, hotel, cultural, parking), transportation hubs (transit at base, office/residential above), institutional complexes.

**Scale Range:** From a 4-story neighborhood mixed-use building (retail + 3 residential floors, 800 m2 GFA) to a supertall mixed-use tower (Hudson Yards 30 HY: 395 m, office + observation + retail). The hybrid stack is the dominant typology for urban mixed-use globally.

**Built Examples:**
- Seattle Central Library (OMA/LMN, 2004) — 5 program platforms (parking, staff, meeting, book spiral, headquarters) in 4 distinct structural zones, each expressed as a different volume in the faceted glass skin, 38,300 m2
- LEGO House, Billund (BIG, 2017) — 21 interlocking rectangular volumes stacked to create rooftop terraces, 12,000 m2, each volume houses a different "zone of play" (red/blue/green/yellow), central "keystone" gallery
- Linked Hybrid, Beijing (Holl, 2009) — 8 towers with stacked programs: commercial podium, residential floors, sky-bridge amenities (pool, gym, gallery) at floors 12-18; each tower has a distinct color/material identity at different levels

### Parti Evaluation Criteria

When choosing between competing parti types for a specific project, evaluate each against these seven criteria:

1. **Program fit:** Does the parti naturally accommodate the area schedule and adjacency requirements? A hospital with 500 beds and 30 departments will not fit in a pinwheel.

2. **Site response:** Does the parti respond to the site's opportunities (views, sun, topography) and constraints (boundaries, easements, neighbors)?

3. **Structural rationality:** Can the parti be supported by an efficient, repetitive structural system? Complex parti types (spiral, folded plate, bridge) cost 15-40% more structurally than simple types (bar, grid, courtyard).

4. **Environmental performance:** Does the parti enable passive strategies? Bars and courtyards are inherently good for daylight and ventilation. Deep slabs and towers require more active systems.

5. **Circulation efficiency:** What is the net-to-gross ratio? Simple bars achieve 82-88% (residential); complex partis (radial, spiral) may drop to 65-72%.

6. **Expandability:** Can the parti grow? Campus and mat partis are infinitely expandable. Towers and centralized plans are fixed.

7. **Cultural legibility:** Does the parti communicate the building's purpose to its community? A courthouse should read as monumental and axial; a children's center should read as playful and clustered.

### Historical Evolution of Parti Thinking

The concept of the parti has evolved significantly across architectural history:

**Beaux-Arts Tradition (1800s-1930s):** The parti was the "big idea" — the axial diagram that governed the entire composition. Students at the Ecole des Beaux-Arts competed in esquisse (sketch) competitions where the parti was established in a single 12-hour session and could not be changed. The parti was understood as a plan composition — typically symmetrical, axial, and hierarchical. Jean-Nicolas-Louis Durand's "Precis des lecons" (1802-1805) codified building types as combinatoric plans: grids, crosses, courtyards, rotundas.

**Modernist Rejection (1920s-1960s):** The early modernists rejected Beaux-Arts parti as formalist and arbitrary. Le Corbusier's "free plan" and Mies's "universal space" appeared to dissolve the parti into fluid, undetermined space. But in reality, Corbusier's promenade architecturale IS a parti (a sequential path through space), and Mies's clear-span pavilion IS a parti (the grid as neutral field). The parti did not disappear; it was redefined.

**Post-Rationalist Revival (1970s-1990s):** Peter Eisenman, John Hejduk, and the New York Five revived the parti as a formal generative device — but now the parti could be diagrammatic, conceptual, or procedural rather than purely spatial. Eisenman's "House" series (I-X) used geometric transformations (rotation, scaling, superposition) as partis. Rem Koolhaas's "programmatic section" (Delirious New York, 1978) proposed the vertical stacking of unrelated programs as a new parti type.

**Computational Era (2000s-present):** Parametric design tools challenge the static parti. Instead of a fixed diagram, the parti becomes a set of rules and relationships (parameters) that generate multiple possible configurations. The designer's role shifts from choosing a parti to designing the parametric system that produces partis. Greg Lynn's "animate form" (1999) argued for a dynamic parti responsive to forces (site, program, structure) rather than a static geometric figure. Despite this, the most successful parametric buildings still have legible partis — the parameters generate form, but the architect curates which generated form embodies the strongest organizational idea.
